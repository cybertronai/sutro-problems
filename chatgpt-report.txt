Why those m values?

For sparse parity, the number of possible hidden subsets is:

$$ N = \binom{n}{k} $$

Each random training example cuts the set of wrong candidate parities by about half. So the critical sample scale is:

$$ m \approx \log_2 \binom{n}{k} $$

Then you choose m slightly below, near, or above that transition.

A rough rule:

target	choose m around
low-but-above-chance	log2(C(n,k)) - 3 or -4
medium	log2(C(n,k)) - 1
high	log2(C(n,k)) + 1
very high / near-perfect	log2(C(n,k)) + 2 or +3
unique recovery with high probability	log2(C(n,k)) + 4 or more

That is why the ladder above uses patterns like:

n=8,  k=3: C=56,  log2 C ≈ 5.8  → m = 4, 6, 8

n=16, k=3: C=560, log2 C ≈ 9.1  → m = 8, 10, 12

Those give you a clean transition from underdetermined to mostly solved without making the programs huge.

Your current repo’s medium benchmark is n=8, k=3, 8 train / 64 test; the README reports medium submissions from 26 ms for try-each-candidate down to 2.7 ms for the best packed submission, so I’ll use those as rough timing anchors. My recommendation

For the main benchmark, use something like:

That is heavy, but plausible for final ranking of top submissions. It is too much for every quick iteration.

Statistical sample size for 42.5% efficiency

I am interpreting “42.5% efficient” as the normalized score:

$$ e = 2\cdot \text{raw accuracy} - 1 $$

So:

$$ e = 0.425 $$

corresponds to raw accuracy:

$$ p = \frac{1+e}{2} = 0.7125 $$

If test-label samples were IID Bernoulli trials, the lower-bound sample size for estimating normalized efficiency within

$$ \pm 0.001 $$

with 99% confidence would be:

$$ N \approx \frac{z_{0.995}^2 \cdot p(1-p)}{(0.001/2)^2} \approx 5.44\text{ million test-label evaluations} $$

That is the optimistic label-level number.

But sparse parity has a stronger task-level correlation: for a given training set, the learner often either recovers the secret or does not. If it recovers, test accuracy is 100%; if it chooses a wrong parity, random test-row accuracy is about 50%. So for equal train/test size m, a better approximation is:

$$ \operatorname{Var}(Z) \approx q(1-q) + \frac{1-q}{m} $$

where:

$$ q = 0.425 $$

and Z is the normalized score of one whole task input.

I would not retain the earlier requirement that training and test sizes be equal. They play unrelated roles:

\(m_{\text{train}}\) controls identifiability and decoding difficulty.

Why \(n=12,\;k=3,\;m_{\text{train}}=8\) is unusually clean

There are

$$ N_{\text{secret}}=\binom{12}{3}=220 $$

possible hidden parities, requiring

$$ \log_2 220 \approx 7.78 $$

bits to identify. Therefore eight binary training labels are almost exactly the minimum plausible amount of information.

There is also a useful boundary:

$$ \binom{12}{3}=220 < 256, \qquad \binom{13}{3}=286 > 256. $$

Thus \(n=12\) is the largest three-sparse problem whose candidate identity can, in principle, fit into an 8-bit training signature. That lines up perfectly with the benchmark’s 8-bit instruction model.

The current packed solver packs eight training labels and each eight-entry training column into one byte, then checks candidate triples using pair-XOR reuse. Nine training examples would cross a real algorithmic boundary: the same method would require multiple packed words.

Why not \(n=8\)?

The current medium problem has

$$ n=8,\quad k=3,\quad m_{\text{train}}=8,\quad m_{\text{test}}=64. $$

Because \(m_{\text{train}}=n\), an ordinary GF(2) linear solver can often reconstruct the entire parity vector without making important use of sparsity. Indeed, the repository already has a Gaussian-elimination baseline for this reason. The sparse structure is not doing enough work.

For \(n=12,m=8\), a full-rank training matrix spans only

$$ \frac{2^8}{2^{12}}=\frac1{16} $$

of the test-input space. A generic linear interpolator can determine labels exactly on that \(1/16\), but outside the span it can only guess. Its expected raw accuracy is therefore approximately

$$ \frac1{16}\cdot1+\frac{15}{16}\cdot\frac12 = \frac{17}{32} = 53.125\%. $$

That gives a useful progression:

$$ 50\% \quad\text{trivial guessing} $$ $$ 53.1\% \quad\text{generic linear interpolation} $$ $$ \text{intermediate values} \quad\text{partial sparse decoding} $$ $$ 100\% \quad\text{complete sparse recovery}. $$

That is a much more informative curve than the current \(n=m=8\) setup.

Keep the unique-identifiability condition

For \(220\) candidates and eight random training examples, a particular false candidate matches all labels with probability \(2^{-8}\). The expected number of false matching candidates is approximately

$$ \lambda=\frac{219}{256}\approx0.855. $$

A Poisson approximation gives

$$ P(\text{unique})\approx e^{-0.855}\approx42.5\%. $$

Thus rejection sampling takes only about

$$ \frac1{0.425}\approx2.35 $$

draws on average. This happens while creating the evaluation suite, not during submission execution.

The current generator already rejects training sets until exactly one \(k\)-subset matches. I would preserve this.

Without conditioning on uniqueness, the maximum attainable score becomes partly an information-theoretic property of the sampled dataset. For these parameters, selecting an arbitrary consistent candidate would produce only about 83–84% expected raw accuracy. That would prevent a clean curve extending through 90–100% and would conflate:

insufficient information in the dataset, and

For this benchmark, the second is what you want to measure.

Why only 32 test examples per invocation?

A large test set looks attractive statistically, but it changes what is being optimized.

Suppose the learner spends energy \(D\) decoding the secret and \(P\) producing every test prediction. An approximate program can:

search only a fraction \(r\) of candidate secrets;
compute only a fraction \(f\) of test outputs;
use a default guess for everything else.

With balanced labels, the normalized advantage over random guessing is approximately

$$ \eta = 2a-1 \approx r f, $$

where \(a\) is raw accuracy. Energy is approximately

$$ E(r,f)\approx E_0+rD+fP. $$

If \(P\gg D\), the obvious strategy is:

decode the secret completely;
calculate only enough outputs to meet the threshold;
guess the rest.

That produces a mostly trivial linear frontier. The current half-output submission illustrates exactly this strategy: it decodes fully, computes 32 of 64 outputs, defaults the rest to zero, and obtains roughly 75% expected accuracy.

At 32 test examples, \(D\approx P\). Then the simple constrained optimum has approximately

$$ r\approx f\approx\sqrt{\eta}. $$

The algorithm must trade off better learning and more predictions throughout the curve. Neither side dominates.

At 128 or 256 test examples, prediction energy overwhelmingly dominates, so much of the leaderboard would likely consist of different ways of truncating the output loop.

Constructing a deterministic test suite

For each secret \(S\):

Derive a deterministic secret-specific permutation of the integers \(0,\ldots,4095\).
Interpret each integer as one 12-bit test row.
Split the permutation into blocks of 32.
Associate each block with a deterministic, independently generated, uniquely identifying training set.

For the ordinary 32-repetition final suite, use 1,024 distinct test rows per secret. To make the trivial baseline exactly 50%, select 512 complement pairs

$$ x,\quad x\oplus(1,1,\ldots,1). $$

Because \(k=3\) is odd,

$$ y(x\oplus \mathbf 1)=y(x)\oplus1. $$

Therefore every complement pair has exactly one zero and one one. Put the two members in different invocations so a submitted program cannot cheaply exploit the pairing inside a single instance.

For the 128-repetition audit, use the entire Boolean cube. Then every nonzero parity is exactly balanced, and any fixed incorrect parity agrees with the true parity on exactly half the inputs.

The current scorer chooses fresh canonical seeds using secrets.SystemRandom() and applies the accuracy threshold separately to every hidden instance. That is good for unpredictability, but not for deterministic leaderboard comparisons.

Derive each instance from something such as

The private key prevents hardcoding. Keeping it fixed prevents two submissions from being ranked on different random draws.

$$ a\ge A. $$

$$ E^\star(A) = \min_{\text{submission}:a\ge A} E. $$
