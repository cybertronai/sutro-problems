The Simplified Dally Model

Arithmetic is essentially free. Moving data is what costs energy. Bill Dally (On the Model of Computation, CACM 2022) proposed pricing algorithms by their data movement on a Manhattan grid — here is the simplest possible version of that idea, one add at a time.

The setup

A single processor sits at the bottom. Above it is a 2D grid of memory cells that can hold numbers. Every number enters and leaves the processor through its intake point — the teal dot sitting on the grid — and moving a number between the intake point and a cell costs the Manhattan distance between them: the number travels vertically first, then horizontally. That L-shaped path is exactly what the diagrams draw, so the picture and the price always agree.

Priced

Reads. Fetching a number into the processor costs its Manhattan distance.

Priced

Writes. Storing a number back to a cell costs the same Manhattan distance.

Free

Arithmetic. Once operands are in registers, computing on them costs nothing.

Worked example: 1 + 7

Two numbers sit out in memory: a 1 and a 7. The processor fetches each one into a register, adds them for free, and pays once more to store the result 8 back to memory. Step through it:

The bill

Why it matters: the add itself was free, yet the computation cost 9 units — every unit was data movement. Scale this intuition up and it explains why real hardware spends most of its energy on wires, caches and DRAM rather than on ALUs, and why good algorithms are the ones that keep their operands close.
